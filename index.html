Theory

Fundamental principles governing thermal energy dissipation

"The rate of loss of heat of a body is directly proportional to the difference between the temperature of the body and the temperature of the surroundings, provided the temperature difference is small."

Integrated form:

Variable Definitions

  • $T$ = temperature of body at time $t$ (°C)
  • $T_0$ = initial temperature of body (°C)
  • $T_s$ = surrounding/ambient temperature (°C)
  • $k$ = cooling constant (min⁻¹)
  • $t$ = elapsed time (minutes)

Physical Behavior

Initially, cooling is faster due to a high thermal gradient. As the temperature of the body approaches room temperature, cooling slows down asymptotically, eventually reaching equilibrium with the surroundings ($T \to T_s$).

Newton's Law of Cooling is a simplified mathematical consequence of Fourier's Law of thermal conduction and Newton's law of convective heat transfer. It assumes that the heat transfer coefficient is uniform across the surface and that the internal thermal resistance of the body is negligible compared to the boundary layer thermal resistance (low Biot number condition).

In real laboratory conditions, radiation losses governed by the Stefan-Boltzmann law also contribute at higher temperatures, but for small temperature excesses above ambient, the linear approximation holds remarkably well.

Precautions

Important experimental guidelines for accurate thermal measurements

  • The surrounding temperature should remain approximately constant throughout the duration of the experiment.
  • Temperature readings must be taken at regular, consistent time intervals.
  • The initial temperature difference between the body and surroundings should preferably be sufficiently high for clear observation.
  • The thermometer bulb should be completely immersed in the liquid sample for accurate thermal sensing.
  • Avoid sudden environmental drafts or temperature fluctuations during the simulation run.
  • Maintain proper calibration parameters when switching sample liquids.
